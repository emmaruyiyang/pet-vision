Each scene was shown under three conditions: a photograph taken at human eye height, one taken at the dog's eye height, and that second one run through a dichromat transform. Two views of the same trial — the aggregate heat map, which shows where the time went, and the live scanpath, which keeps the order it went in. The numbers under each plate are the same in both.

How to read a plate In heat map view the trial is summed over its whole duration, normalised within its own plate — colour says where he looked in that plate, never how much compared with the plate beside it. In scanpath view the same trial runs in time: the bright point is where gaze is now and the trail behind it fades, the ring grows when gaze holds still (roughly, a fixation) and collapses to a thin line during a saccade, and a panel whose trial has ended draws its whole path. How to read the numbers The stripe is that trial's dwell split across classes. Each row gives the class's dwell share and, on the right, dwell minus the share of the image that class covers, in percentage points: +31 means it took 31 points more of his time than its area alone would predict, −22 means that much less. Areas come from ADE20K segmentation, where ground excludes grass — grass is filed under vegetation. Rows are the top five classes reaching 1% dwell. Read with the error in mind Gaze is placed on the stimulus by a homography estimated from the monitor's own outline, not from AprilTags, so a fixation is good to tens of stimulus pixels rather than single ones. The rectified screen comes out at aspect 1.43 against the stimulus's 1.78, so vertical positions in this session are compressed by about 20%; horizontal is unaffected. n = 1 — this is what one person did, not what people do.
